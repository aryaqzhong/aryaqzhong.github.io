What to listen for

  • B vs. C (same 5.8 ms latency): the low-frequency body of the voice and how much accompaniment leaks through. On the 50 MUSDB18-HQ test songs, C's median SI-SDR is 1.03 dB higher than B's (4.20 vs. 3.17 dB); compared song by song, the median improvement is 0.76 dB (95% CI 0.57 to 0.90), and C is better on 45 of the 50 songs (figure below).
  • E vs. D (same 2.9 ms latency): D reaches 4.02 dB median SI-SDR vs. E's 3.17 dB. Compared song by song, the median gain is 0.73 dB (95% CI 0.59 to 0.88), with D better on 45 of 50 songs. Halving the dual window's latency from C to D costs a median 0.16 dB per song (95% CI 0.11 to 0.24); C is better on 39 of 50 songs.
  • The low end of the voice: with simulated bleed, B loses the most vocal energy in the 125–250 Hz octave (3.6 dB, vs. 2.6 dB for C and 2.4 dB for A). B's 256-sample window puts its frequency bins 172 Hz apart (C's analysis window: 21.5 Hz), but resolution alone does not explain the ordering: E's even shorter window loses 2.9 dB there, and D 2.2 dB.
  • A (23.2 ms) is the long-window reference; C recovers most of the gap between B and A.
  • HTDemucs is a large offline model that sees seconds of future audio (8.49 dB SI-SDR on the same test songs with the same mono input). It shows what is possible without a latency constraint, not a competitor at these latencies.
  • Vocal mic with bleed: a simulated stage situation, the accompaniment leaking into the vocal microphone 5 dB below the voice. All four songs have supplied vocal and accompaniment stems; this scenario compares A through E. At 2.9 ms and equal 8.9 dB bleed attenuation, D loses 0.67 dB of vocal energy vs. E's 0.97 dB in the 50-song benchmark.

Is the improvement consistent across songs?

Per-song difference between the dual and the short window on the 50 test songs: C − B at 5.8 ms and D − E at 2.9 ms. The dual window improves 45 of 50 songs at each latency.

The gain is not universal. In a post-hoc analysis of the worst 2.9 ms case, a song with very quiet vocals, D suppresses more accompaniment but also removes more target vocal energy than E. The failure persists when scoring vocal-active intervals. The full 50-song results above remain the primary comparison.

The models

Test-set SI-SDR: median over the 50 MUSDB18-HQ test songs (mono, full length), two training seeds averaged. Compute: full streaming pipeline (STFT, network, iSTFT) on one CPU thread with ONNX Runtime; the 99th-percentile time per hop and the share of hops that took longer than the hop show whether every audio callback meets its deadline, which the mean real-time factor alone does not. At 2.9 ms algorithmic latency, D takes 0.76 ms at the 99th percentile per 1.45 ms hop (real-time factor 0.515) with ONNX Runtime.

Beyond direct mixing

With frozen models and accompaniment re-normalized to 5 dB below the clean vocal, the paired D-over-E SI-SDR gain is 0.78 dB under fixed filtering and delay, and 0.91 dB under a synthetic-room path (50 songs, two seeds). These mild synthetic paths preserve the gain; they are not real-microphone or acoustic-feedback tests.

A separate experiment widened the short-window GRU until its model MACs matched the dual-window models. With a learning rate chosen on validation scores, the wider short-window models reach 3.63 dB at 5.8 ms and 3.41 dB at 2.9 ms; the dual window still leads by a median 0.51 dB per song (95% CI 0.40 to 0.65; better on 42 of 50 songs) and 0.50 dB (95% CI 0.28 to 0.63; 41 of 50). Matched MACs are not matched runtime: the wider 2.9 ms model misses 52.7% of its deadlines. The listening buttons above use the original A–E models; the wider controls are reported in the project results.

Notes

  • Algorithmic latency is set by the synthesis window (M samples, worst case M − 1). It was checked on the implementation: a prefix test with the trained models (largest measured look-ahead 247 samples for B and C, bound 255; 123 samples for D and E, bound 127) and an impulse test of the frame-by-frame code. End-to-end latency through an audio interface adds driver buffering and was not measured. Timings come from Python on a shared server; a deployed system would run in a dedicated real-time audio thread.
  • The demo audio uses excerpts of songs from ccMixter under Creative Commons Attribution licenses (credits above the player). Changes: the mixes were converted to mono, excerpted, separated by the models listed here and loudness-matched. No MUSDB18 audio is used on this page.
  • Code and results: the project README.
