Beyond Words

When pianists play the same notes, which of their differences do experts’ words capture, and which consistent playing habits do they miss?

Qing (Arya) Zhongread the study ↓listening room ↓code ↗

Abstract

Piano performances are judged in words such as “bright–dark” or “dry–wet pedal”, and perception models are trained on those ratings. Using PercePiano1, I compare different pianists playing the same notes and ask what its 19 rating scales capture and what they miss. Experts reliably separate pianists on pedaling, pace and timing, but barely on brightness or loudness. Interpretable MIDI features explain up to 62% of what is explainable. Two playing habits recur for each pianist, yet no scale and no single expert predicts them, and an open piano renderer drops one of them entirely. Four of the 19 scales recover most of what all 19 measure.

1 · Background and motivation

Nineteen words for one performance

Competitions, teachers and listening studies describe piano playing in words. PercePiano1 turned this into data: experts rated performances from the Yamaha e-Competition on 19 seven-point scales, and models are trained to predict those ratings from the playing2.

A model can only learn what the ratings contain, and two things about them are unknown. Some scales may be mostly rating noise, which no model can learn. And pianists may differ in ways no scale describes, which a model trained on the ratings never learns.

Prior work predicts the ratings. This project also asks the reverse question: which consistent differences between pianists do the ratings leave out?

3 · Significance

Know what a rating can carry

Rating scales connect how music is played with how it is heard. Their limits matter to anyone who builds on them.

Perception models

A scale’s noise ceiling bounds any model trained on it, and a habit outside the vocabulary is never learned.

Questionnaire design

Fewer, reliable scales save the raters’ time, if the short form keeps the signal.

Listening studies

A blind spot becomes a testable hypothesis, as long as the playback reproduces the cue.

4 · Method

Hold the notes, compare the playing

Noise ceilingThe share of the rating differences between pianists in a passage that is not rating noise: the most any model of that scale can explain.

Every analysis compares pianists who played the same passage, so differences in the notes cannot explain differences in the ratings. From there the study works in two directions, each tested on pianists the model has not seen.

1 / 4 · Design

Same notes, different playing.

About twelve pianists play each passage. The notes are identical; timing, loudness and the two pedals are not. Features and ratings are compared within the passage.

2 / 4 · Noise ceiling

First: how much of a rating is signal?

A rater model removes each expert’s leniency. The agreement that remains is the noise ceiling, the bar every later result is measured against.

3 / 4 · Direction A

Which features explain each scale?

21 interpretable MIDI features, such as tempo against the score, sustain depth and key velocity, predict each scale in ridge regression, with each pianist held out in turn.

4 / 4 · Direction B

Which habits does no scale predict?

Reversed, the 19 ratings predict each playing habit. A habit is a blind spot if it is consistent for a pianist (performer ICC ≥ 0.30) but not predicted (R² ≤ 0.10). The rule was fixed before the results were seen.

5 · Data and experimental design

PercePiano, rebuilt from single ratings

PercePiano1 holds MIDI performances by 20 pianists from the Yamaha e-Competition. Each of 99 short passages from Beethoven and Schubert is played by about twelve of them, and each performance was rated by 9–10 experts.

20pianists
99passages
19rating scales
1,073performances
Performer ICCHow consistent a habit is for one pianist across passages: the share of its variance that belongs to the pianist.

Before any modeling, the labels were rebuilt from the individual ratings:

  • “I don’t know” answers count as missing, not as a rating.
  • Repeated submissions by one rater count once; 3,060 rater–performance pairs had repeats.
  • Two separate rater pools rated different works, so every comparison stays within a passage.

6 · Results

Five findings

Each part answers one row of Table 1 and opens with its finding.

6.1What experts agree on

Finding 1Experts reliably separate pianists on pedaling, pace and timing, with noise ceilings of 0.62–0.83, but barely on brightness, valence, energy and loudness, at 0.14–0.23.

Nineteen scales for one performance.

Each performance was rated by 9–10 experts on every scale, from pedal amount and pace to interpretation and valence.

Raw agreement varies widely.

How consistently the experts separate pianists within a passage ranges from near zero to about 0.8, before any correction.

With leniency removed.

The ceiling is 0.62–0.83 for pedal, pace and timing, and only 0.14–0.23 for brightness, valence, energy and loudness. On those four, a rating says little about the pianist.

Fig. 2the noise ceiling of each scale

Within-passage reliability per scale. Source ↗

Part of the noise is simply the number of raters. The dataset paper reports reliabilities of 0.92–0.98, which by the Spearman–Brown formula correspond to about 63 raters per performance. A performance actually has 9–10.

6.2What the playing explains

Finding 2Playing features explain pace and pedaling best: pace reaches R² = 0.43, 62% of its ceiling. Interpretation, valence and balance stay below 20% of theirs.

What the notes explain.

The 21 features predict each scale for pianists held out of training. White: R² for unseen pianists. Gray: the ceiling.

Pace: 62% of what can be explained.

Pace reaches R² = 0.43. Pedal features carry pedal amount and clarity, touch, articulation and timbre depth. For timing, tempo measured against the score explains 0.28, against 0.15 without the score.

Evaluative words stay out of reach.

Interpretation, valence and balance stay below 20% of their ceilings: summaries of the notes say little about them.

R² from leave-one-pianist-out ridge regression. Source ↗

6.3What the words miss

Finding 3Two habits are consistent for each pianist yet invisible in the ratings: soft-pedal use and pedal re-articulation, with performer ICC ≈ 0.31 and R² ≈ 0. No single expert’s ratings track soft-pedal use.

Most habits leave a trace.

Tempo against the score, sustain depth and key velocity are consistent for a pianist and well predicted from the ratings.

Two do not.

Soft-pedal use and pedal re-articulation, how often the sustain pedal is changed, recur across passages, yet the 19 ratings predict neither.

Not even a minority of experts.

For each of 47 experts separately, their own ratings predict tempo for 35 and sustain depth for 26, but pedal re-articulation for 3 and soft-pedal use for none.

6.4The playback can drop the cue

Finding 4The experts heard a virtual-piano rendering of the MIDI. An open renderer ignores the soft pedal completely: 711 of 734 renders come out sample-identical without it.

The ratings do not reflect the two habits. That does not mean listeners cannot hear them.

734 performances, rendered twice.

Every performance with soft-pedal events was rendered as played, and again with all soft-pedal events removed (FluidSynth, Yamaha C5 grand samples).

711 come out sample-identical.

The other 23 match up to where the shorter file ends. A playback chain can silently remove the very cue of a blind spot.

The sustain pedal is different.

Removing the sustain pedal instead changes 666 of the 734 renders. Where audio carries real information it adds to MIDI: R² for dynamic range rises from 0.08 to 0.15.

6.5Four scales carry most of the signal

Finding 5Pedal amount, interpretation, timing and pace recover 91% of what all 19 scales measure about the experts’ consensus. Chosen on training pieces only, the same four are selected in all 25 held-out folds.

Several scales are interchangeable at the consensus level: space, drama, imagination and timbre variety correlate at about 1. The questionnaire measures fewer distinct qualities than it has scales.

7 · Conclusion

The words hear the pedal and the pace; two habits slip through

RQ1

How consistently do experts separate pianists?

Reliably on pedaling, pace and timing; barely on brightness, valence, energy and loudness. With 9–10 raters, 11 scales cannot compare single performances.

RQ2

What in the playing explains each scale?

Tempo and pedal features explain pace and the pedal scales, up to 62% of the ceiling; evaluative scales stay below 20%.

RQ3

Which consistent habits do the scales miss?

Soft-pedal use and pedal re-articulation: predicted by no scale and by no single expert, and a renderer can remove the first entirely.

For anyone who evaluates performances in words or trains models on the ratings: check each scale’s ceiling before modeling, consider a short form of four scales, and test habits outside the vocabulary with listeners, on a playback chain that reproduces them.

7.1Limitations

One dataset
19 scales, 20 pianists and three works, with one rater pool per work.
MIDI, not sound
MIDI does not record touch such as key acceleration3, and the experts’ rendering is not public.
Ratings, not perception
A blind spot shows what the ratings do not reflect, not what listeners cannot hear.
Internal validation
The short form was checked on held-out pieces of the same dataset, and its intervals are wide.

7.2Next: a listening test

A paired test can settle what the ratings cannot. Listeners rate the same performance twice, as played and with only the pedal changed. Power was simulated with listeners and stimuli both random4 and with PercePiano’s own rating noise.
