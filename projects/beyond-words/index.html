Piano performance and perception

Beyond Words

What expert ratings of piano performances capture about how pianists play, and which consistent playing habits they miss.

Qing (Arya) Zhong2026Listening room ↓Code ↗

I · Data

Same notes, different playing

PercePiano collects performances by 20 pianists from the Yamaha e-Competition. Short passages are each played by about twelve of them and rated by experts on 19 seven-point scales: “bright–dark”, “dry–wet pedal”, “stable–unstable timing” and so on.

Every comparison here is between pianists playing the same passage, so the notes are fixed and only the playing differs. The labels were rebuilt from the individual ratings: “I don’t know” answers are left out and repeated submissions by one rater count once.

II · Noise ceiling

How consistent are the experts?

Before asking what explains a rating, it helps to know how much of it is signal. A rater model separates each expert’s leniency from real differences between pianists; the agreement that remains is the noise ceiling, the most any model of these ratings could explain.

Vocabulary

Nineteen words for one performance.

Each performance was rated by 9–10 experts on every scale, from pedal amount and pace to interpretation and valence.

Raw agreement varies widely.

How consistently the experts separate pianists within a passage ranges from near zero to about 0.8, before any correction.

With leniency removed.

The noise ceiling is 0.62–0.83 for pedal, pace and timing, but only 0.14–0.23 for brightness, valence, energy and loudness. On those four, single ratings say little about the pianist.

IV · Blind spots

Habits nobody rates

Now turn the question around: which consistent playing habits do the 19 scales fail to predict? Each habit is placed by how consistent it is for a pianist across passages, and by how well the 19 ratings predict it for pianists the model has not seen.

Most habits leave a trace.

Tempo against the score, sustain depth and key velocity are consistent for a pianist and well predicted from the ratings.

Two do not.

Soft-pedal use and pedal re-articulation recur across passages (performer ICC ≈ 0.31), yet the ratings predict neither (R² ≈ 0). The rules of this screen were fixed before looking at the results.

Not even a minority of experts.

For each of 47 experts separately, their own ratings predict tempo for 35 and sustain depth for 26, but pedal re-articulation for 3 and soft-pedal use for none.

This shows that the ratings do not reflect these habits, not that listeners cannot hear them.

Performer ICC across passages; out-of-fold R² from the 19 ratings. Per-expert tests use held-out pianists and within-passage permutations, FDR-corrected.

V · Audio

The renderer drops the soft pedal

The experts heard a virtual-piano rendering of the MIDI, not a recording. Rendering every performance with an open sampler (FluidSynth, with Yamaha C5 grand and Kawai upright samples) shows how fragile that playback chain can be.

734 performances, rendered twice.

Every performance with soft-pedal events was rendered as played, and again with all soft-pedal events removed.

711 come out sample-identical.

The other 23 are identical up to where the shorter file ends. The renderer ignores the soft pedal entirely: a playback chain can silently remove the very cue of the blind spot.

The sustain pedal is different.

Removing the sustain pedal instead changes 666 of the 734 renders. Where audio carries real information it adds to MIDI: R² for dynamic range rises from 0.08 to 0.15 with the grand.

Fig. va contact sheet of renders

Squares are counts, not specific performances. White: sample-identical; gray: identical until the shorter file ends; dark: changed.

VI · Next

Testing it with listeners

The ratings cannot settle whether the two habits are audible. A paired listening test can: each listener rates the same performance twice, once as played and once with only the pedal changed, while the notes stay identical.

How many listeners?

Power is simulated for the planned design, with listeners and stimuli as random effects and PercePiano’s own rating noise.

Timbre depth: 29 listeners.

To detect half the typical difference between pianists in timbre depth with 80% power takes 29 listeners with 24 stimulus pairs, or 67 with 12.

Pedal clarity: 13.

Pedal clarity needs 13 listeners with 24 pairs. The original design, with different performances rated by about ten raters each, has 6% power for the same timbre-depth difference.

Limitations

  • A public vocabulary of 19 scales, 20 pianists and three works.
  • The experts heard one rendering; the open renders here are not that instrument.
  • MIDI does not record touch details such as key acceleration.
  • The listening test is designed and powered, not yet run.
