DualWindow-MSS

How much vocal quality can a streaming separator recover when it has only a few milliseconds to respond? An experimental study of window design, model capacity and compute.

Qing (Arya) Zhongread the study ↓try the demo ↓code ↗

Abstract

Live sound needs the voice separated from the band within a few milliseconds, yet a short STFT window blurs the spectrum a separator relies on. I test a dual-window STFT: a 2048-sample analysis window for spectral detail and a short synthesis window that keeps the algorithmic latency at 5.8 or 2.9 ms. With the same small network it improves 45 of 50 test songs at each latency, stays within its latency bound and runs in real time; one very quiet song gets much worse.

1 · Motivation

Separating a voice while the music is still arriving

Algorithmic latencyThe delay the algorithm itself imposes: how long it must wait for input before it can produce the matching output. Computation time and audio buffers come on top.

Music source separation pulls one source, here a singer’s voice, out of a mixture. Offline separators such as HTDemucs [4] read seconds of audio around each moment. A live system cannot wait: in in-ear monitoring or vocal-microphone processing, every millisecond of delay reaches the performer.

Frequency binsAn STFT of N samples at 44.1 kHz measures frequencies 44100 / N Hz apart: 43 Hz for 1024 samples, 345 Hz for 128.

Most separators work on a short-time Fourier transform, and there the window sets the wait. Shortening it to 5.8 or 2.9 ms leaves frequency bins 172 or 345 Hz apart: too coarse to keep a voice’s harmonics apart from the band’s.

At the same 5.8 or 2.9 ms latency, can a long analysis window recover the vocal quality a short window loses, and what does it cost?

If it can, the window becomes a design lever for any STFT separator, independent of the network. Three hypotheses structure the study:

H1 · Quality

A long analysis window with a short synthesis window recovers quality at the short window’s latency.

H2 · Cost

The longer analysis costs compute, not latency.

H3 · Capacity

The gain does not depend on a large network.

3 · Method

Analyze a long past, answer with a short window

3.1The dual window

Following Mauler and Martin [1], the window’s two jobs are split: the analysis length N decides how much detail the model sees, the synthesis length M how long the output waits. Scroll through the figure, then try any pair in Fig. 3.

Fig. 2 · a window is a wait

Synthetic two-tone illustration; spectra normalized to show their shape. Bars show sample ranges, not window weights. Window construction ↗

1 / 4 · Detail

A long window resolves two nearby tones.

A 220 Hz voice tone and a 330 Hz band tone, seen through a 1024-sample window: bins every 43 Hz and two clear peaks. But the window must fill before it can be processed: 23.2 ms of waiting.

2 / 4 · The trade-off

A short window waits less and sees less.

At 128 samples the wait drops to 2.9 ms, but the bins are 345 Hz apart and the tones merge (at 256 samples, 5.8 ms, they are 172 Hz apart). In a symmetric STFT, latency and detail are locked together.

3 / 4 · The idea

Give analysis and synthesis different jobs.

The analysis window reads the last 2048 samples, which have already arrived; the synthesis window keeps only the last 128. The model sees bins 22 Hz apart, and the output still waits only 2.9 ms.

4 / 4 · Reconstruction

Nothing is lost on the way out.

The two windows multiply to a 128-sample Hann window. Overlapped every 64 samples these frames add up to one, so an unchanged spectrum reconstructs the input exactly.

3.2The separator

All five configurations share one compact network, 0.94 M parameters, trained separately for each window pair. A convolutional encoder runs along frequency, pools to 64 bands and feeds one causal GRU; a mask head outputs a gain between 0 and 1 per bin, so the system can only keep or remove energy from the mixture.

Bootstrap intervalResample the 50 songs with replacement 10,000 times and recompute the paired median each time; the middle 95% of those medians is the interval.

Every comparison is paired per song: the two seeds are averaged, one model is subtracted from the other on the same song, and the median of the 50 differences is reported with a song-bootstrap 95% interval and the number of songs that improve. Medians, because a few songs have almost no vocal and would dominate a mean.

Training details
  • Two seeds per configuration; 200,000 updates on 3-second crops, batch size 16.
  • AdamW at 10−3 with 2,000 warmup updates and cosine decay; the best full-song validation checkpoint, checked every 5,000 updates, is kept.
  • Augmentation: random stem gains, cross-song remixing and 20% vocal-dominant mixtures.
  • Loss on the reconstructed waveform: 10 × L1 plus a multi-resolution spectral term (FFT sizes 512, 1024, 2048), the same for every window, so changing the window does not change the objective.
  • The matched-compute learning-rate checks were prompted by test results and reuse the test songs, so they are exploratory. Configuration ↗ · Follow-up record ↗

5 · Results

Five findings

Each part answers one row of Table 1 and opens with its finding.

5.1Quality at the same latency

Finding 1At both latencies the dual window improves 45 of 50 songs: a median +0.76 dB at 5.8 ms and +0.73 dB at 2.9 ms.

Fig. 5 · 45 of 50 songs improve

One bar per MUSDB18-HQ test song, two seeds averaged, sorted by gain; bars below −3 dB are clipped. Source ↗

5.8 ms · C vs B

Most songs improve.

Each bar is one test song, raised by the dual window’s gain over the 256-sample window at the same latency. Median +0.76 dB (95% CI 0.57 to 0.90).

2.9 ms · D vs E

The same at half the latency.

Against the 128-sample window: +0.73 dB (0.59 to 0.88), again on 45 of 50 songs. Halving the latency costs the dual window only 0.16 dB.

Controls

Not just more compute or a bigger network.

Short-window models widened to the same multiply-adds still trail by a median 0.50 dB (41 of 50 songs). Shrunk to 0.17 M parameters, the dual window keeps +0.54 dB; in speech the window helped weak models more [3], here slightly less.

Failure case

One quiet song goes the other way.

On PR – Happy Daze the voice sits 26 dB below the band, and the dual window removes much more of the voice: about 20 dB worse. That one song pulls the mean to +0.35 dB; without it the mean is +0.77 dB, while the median barely moves. The gain holds for most songs, not on average.

5.2Why it works

Ideal maskA mask computed from the true vocal and accompaniment stems: the best any masking network could do with a given window pair.

Finding 2The window sets the ceiling. With ideal masks, a long analysis window recovers detail at 5.8 ms that even a symmetric window at twice the latency lacks; the trained models realize about 40% of that potential.

Fig. 6 · the window sets the ceiling

Ideal ratio masks, uSDR on 30-second excerpts of the 14 validation songs; the gain comparison uses full test songs. Source ↗

Short windows cap the quality.

With ideal masks, symmetric windows lose about 3 dB from 23.2 to 2.9 ms, before any network is trained.

A long analysis window lifts the cap.

At 5.8 ms, a 2048-sample analysis window reaches 10.06 dB, above a symmetric window at twice the latency (9.52 dB). Going to 4096 adds nothing, so 2048 was chosen, on validation songs only.

The trained models realize about 40%.

On the test songs the ideal-mask gain at 5.8 ms is +1.87 dB, positive on every song; the trained models reach +0.76 dB. The rest is room for a better estimator.

5.3The latency is real

Finding 3The long analysis window adds no delay: the trained models look at most 123 samples ahead at 2.9 ms, under the bound of 127.

Fig. 7 · 123 ≤ 127 samples

Schematic; the measured look-ahead is the largest over 16 change positions within a hop, with the trained weights. Source ↗

Two inputs that agree, then differ.

Feed the trained model two signals that are identical up to sample n. Any output that changes before n reveals how far the model looks ahead.

Where the outputs part is the look-ahead.

The largest look-ahead is 123 samples at 2.9 ms (bound 127) and 247 at 5.8 ms (bound 255). An impulse through the streaming code comes out exactly one hop later. Audio-interface buffers were not measured.

5.4Fast enough for live audio

Real-time factorProcessing time divided by audio duration. Below 1 the system keeps up on average; the 99th percentile shows whether individual hops do.

Finding 4The dual window keeps up in real time: at 2.9 ms it needs 0.76 ms of each 1.45 ms hop at the 99th percentile, and 0.01% of hops were late.

A new 64-sample block arrives every 1.45 ms and the previous one must be finished by then. The full pipeline was timed with ONNX Runtime on one CPU thread.

Fig. 8 · half the time budget

one slot = one 1.45 ms hoptime spent computing itfinished after the deadline

Mean time per hop from the benchmark (one Xeon Gold 6150 thread), shown in slow motion. Source ↗

Half the budget.

Each slot is one hop; the bar is the compute for that block. The dual-window model finishes in 0.75 ms on average.

The price is compute, not latency.

The 128-sample window alone needs half that time: real-time factor 0.515 against 0.262 at the same latency.

The runtime matters too.

The same model in eager PyTorch takes 1.7 ms per hop and misses every deadline; a live system needs an optimized runtime.

5.5A leaky vocal microphone

Finding 5In a vocal microphone with the band leaking in, the dual window keeps more of the voice: removing the same 8.9 dB of band, D loses 0.67 dB of vocal energy and E 0.97 dB.

The band is mixed 5 dB below the voice. Applying each model’s mask to voice and band separately splits the output into voice kept and band left [6]. A stronger mask removes more of both, so models are compared at equal band removal. The gain also holds when the band passes through filtering, delay or a synthetic room (+0.59 to +0.91 dB).

6 · Conclusion

Spend compute on spectral history

All three hypotheses hold within the tested configurations.

H1

A long analysis window recovers quality at the short window’s latency.

+0.76 / +0.73 dB per song; 45 of 50 songs; still +0.50 dB against matched compute.

H2

It costs compute, not latency.

Look-ahead 123 ≤ 127 and 247 ≤ 255 samples; about 2× the CPU time, within every deadline.

H3

The gain does not need a large network.

+0.54 to +0.76 dB from 0.17 M to 0.94 M parameters.

The analysis window deserves a place next to network size in low-latency design. With the dual window, moving from 23.2 to 5.8 ms costs 0.31 dB per song instead of 1.05 dB, and 2.9 ms costs another 0.16 dB. The evidence is objective and simulated: it does not yet show a perceptual preference or a working live system.

7 · Limitations

Where the evidence ends

One dataset, objective scores
Mono vocals from MUSDB18-HQ, scored with SI-SDR; there is no listening test yet.
A typical-song gain with an exception
At 2.9 ms the mean gain’s interval includes zero because of one very quiet vocal.
Small models, few seeds
Two seeds per configuration; the matched-compute learning-rate checks reuse the test songs and are exploratory.
Simulated, not live
One CPU and no audio-interface latency; the bleed and rooms are digital simulations.

Next: a listening test, end-to-end timing on an audio interface, and recordings from a real stage.
