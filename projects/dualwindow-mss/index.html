Personal research project · 2026

DualWindow-MSS

How much vocal quality can a streaming separator recover when it has only a few milliseconds to respond? An experimental study of window design, model capacity and compute.

Qing (Arya) Zhongread the study ↓try the demo ↓code ↗

Abstract

Live sound needs the singing voice separated from the band within a few milliseconds, but a short-time Fourier transform with such a short window blurs the very spectrum a separator relies on. This study tests a dual-window STFT for causal vocal separation: a 2048-sample analysis window gives the network a detailed view of the recent past, while a short synthesis window keeps the algorithmic latency at 5.8 or 2.9 ms.

With the same compact architecture, the dual window improves vocal SI-SDR over a short symmetric window by a median +0.76 dB per song at 5.8 ms and +0.73 dB at 2.9 ms on the 50 MUSDB18-HQ test songs. The latency bound is verified on the trained models, both dual-window models keep up with real time on one CPU thread, and the gain persists across model sizes, against wider models with matched compute, and in a simulated vocal microphone. One song with a very quiet vocal gets much worse, so the average gain at 2.9 ms remains uncertain.

1 · Background and motivation

Separating a voice while the music is still arriving

Music source separation estimates individual sources from a mixture: for example, a singer’s voice from vocals, drums, bass and other instruments. For a finished recording, a model can inspect audio on both sides of the moment it is reconstructing; offline separators such as HTDemucs [4] read seconds of context. A live system has a different constraint: waiting for future audio delays what the performer hears.

Algorithmic latencyThe delay the algorithm itself imposes: how long it must wait for input before it can produce the matching output. Computation time and audio-interface buffers come on top (section 6.4).

In-ear monitoring and vocal-microphone processing motivate a budget of only a few milliseconds. A useful separator must preserve the voice, suppress accompaniment and finish its computation in time. Good separation quality by itself does not establish that the system can work within that budget.

Frequency binsAn STFT of N samples at 44.1 kHz measures frequencies 44100 / N Hz apart: 43 Hz for 1024 samples, 345 Hz for 128.

Many separators work in a short-time Fourier transform (STFT): they analyze a short stretch of audio as frequency bins, estimate which parts belong to the voice, then reconstruct the waveform. Shortening a conventional window reduces the wait, but also makes nearby vocal and instrumental components harder to distinguish.

This tension between time and frequency resolution is the starting point of my project. I investigate whether the analysis and reconstruction stages need to use the same window length.

2 · Related work

The ideas this project builds on

Long-analysis, short-synthesis windows come from earlier signal-processing research and have been used in neural speech enhancement. Those findings motivate the design here; their outcomes need to be tested in the music-vocal setting.

2007 · Mauler & Martin [1]

Separate spectral resolution from synthesis delay

A longer analysis window and a shorter synthesis window can preserve perfect reconstruction while reducing delay. I use this analysis–synthesis construction as the foundation of the separator.

Read the EUSIPCO paper ↗

2023 · Wang et al. [2]

Use dual windows in neural speech enhancement

Their frame-online enhancement system combines dual-window STFT processing with complex spectral mapping and other techniques, reporting algorithmic latency as low as 2 ms. It demonstrates the approach in speech enhancement and motivates testing a compact causal model for music vocals.

Read the TASLP paper ↗

2025 · Wu & Braun [3]

Check whether the model changes the window’s benefit

In their controlled speech-enhancement study, asymmetric windows clearly helped a weaker U-Net, while gains for the stronger CRUSE model were inconclusive. This motivates my model-size controls. The architectures, tasks and feature construction differ, so the studies’ numbers are not directly comparable.

Read the ICASSP paper ↗

2023 · Rouard et al. [4]

Keep an offline music-separation reference

HTDemucs combines waveform and spectrogram processing with a cross-domain Transformer and seconds-long context. I include it as an offline quality reference, with access to future audio and a different training setup; it is not a latency-matched control.

Read the HTDemucs paper ↗

3 · Research question and significance

What can dual windows buy for music vocals?

A singer and a band share harmonic structure and frequency regions. Evidence from speech enhancement alone does not establish how much a dual window will help separate that mixture, or whether any improvement is worth its extra computation.

At the same 5.8 or 2.9 ms algorithmic latency, can a long analysis window recover vocal quality lost by a short window—and what does that recovery cost?

I built a compact streaming separator and a controlled experimental pipeline to study this question. My contribution is the implementation and evaluation in this setting: separating window effects from model capacity and compute, verifying latency in code, and examining the trade between vocal preservation and accompaniment suppression.

The practical decision is how to spend a constrained processing budget: on more spectral detail, a larger network, or a longer wait. I organize the study around three hypotheses:

H1 · Quality

A long analysis window paired with a short synthesis window recovers quality at the short window’s latency.

H2 · Cost

Extending analysis increases computation while the synthesis window continues to determine algorithmic latency.

H3 · Capacity

The quality gain persists when the separator is made smaller.

The next two sections define the system and the comparisons used to test these hypotheses. Results follow the experimental design.

4.1 · Method · the dual window

Analyze a long past, synthesize a short output

Following Mauler and Martin [1], I separate the window’s two jobs. The analysis length N controls the spectral representation; the synthesis length M controls the output’s algorithmic wait. The analysis window uses samples that have already arrived, while the synthesis window keeps only the recent part of each reconstructed frame.

First, the figure builds up the conventional short-window trade-off. It then introduces the dual window and shows why the overlapping windows still reconstruct an unchanged signal.

1 / The wait

A frame has to arrive before it can be processed.

A conventional STFT uses equally long analysis and synthesis windows. At 44.1 kHz, 1024 samples correspond to about 23.2 ms of algorithmic latency.

The strip represents one frame. Its right edge is the newest sample, now. A shorter frame would reduce the wait—but changes what the model can see.

2 / Frequency detail

First, look at just two tones.

This synthetic example contains a vocal tone at 220 Hz and an accompaniment tone at 330 Hz. The marks above the chart identify the tones; the curve below shows their combined windowed spectrum.

With 1024 samples, the STFT reads a frequency bin every 43 Hz. Those bins are the dots on the curve. The two peaks are distinguishable.

3 / A shorter window

Less waiting, fewer frequency bins.

The tones and the frequency axis stay fixed. Only the window shrinks to 256 samples: about 5.8 ms of wait, with bins now 172 Hz apart.

The two components blur together. A mask must now make its decision from fewer, less distinct measurements.

4 / Shorter still

At 2.9 ms, still less detail.

A 128-sample window brings the wait down to about 2.9 ms. But bins are 345 Hz apart: only two bin centers lie in the displayed 0–600 Hz range.

This is the trade-off the dual window addresses: keep the short output wait while giving analysis a longer view.

5 / Separate the jobs

Read more history; output a short tail.

The upper bar is the 2048-sample analysis range. The lower bar is the 128-sample synthesis range. They share the same time axis and both end at now.

The extra analysis samples are already in the past. They give bins about 22 Hz apart while the short synthesis window keeps the algorithmic wait near 2.9 ms.

6 / Reconstruct the signal

The overlap fills in, rather than piling up.

The two window weights multiply to a 128-sample Hann window. With a new frame every 64 samples, one frame’s contribution fades out as the next fades in.

They are drawn separately here. The bottom row adds them: the weight stays at one. With the spectrum unchanged, the input is reconstructed exactly, apart from the streaming delay.

7 / The comparison to test

Same synthesis window. More spectral detail.

Compare short-window E with dual-window D: the analysis length and frequency spacing change, but the synthesis length stays at 128 samples.

Both have the same framing bound of M − 1 = 127 samples, about 2.88 ms. The later experiments test whether this design improves separation and whether the implementation respects that bound.

4.2 · Method · the separator

One network architecture, five window configurations

Every configuration A–E uses the same pipeline and network architecture, trained separately for its window pair; only the windows and the hop change. The network is deliberately small, 0.94 M parameters, because it has to finish every block before the next one arrives.

A–E are model names. Windows are analysis / synthesis samples; hop = synthesis / 2.

A · long1024 / 102423.2 ms
B · short256 / 2565.8 ms
C · dual2048 / 2565.8 ms
E · short128 / 1282.9 ms
D · dual2048 / 1282.9 ms

Fig. 4 · the pipeline

Shown: D at 2.9 ms, with one 64-sample block per hop. Stages light up as the text describes them. The final compute comparison uses C and B, both at 5.8 ms. Source ↗

Blocks in, blocks out.

Audio arrives in blocks of 64 samples (one hop, 1.45 ms at 2.9 ms latency). Each new block is appended to a buffer of the recent past, and the chain runs once.

The window pair sets the framing.

The analysis window reads the last 2048 samples of the buffer and produces 1025 frequency bins. The synthesis window, used at the far end, covers only the last 128 samples: that is the only part of the output that has to wait.

A small, causal network.

A convolutional encoder runs along frequency with weights shared across bins, pools to 64 bands, and one causal GRU layer carries context forward in time, never backward. Weights are shared across frequency bins within each model; each window configuration is trained separately.

A mask, not a new signal.

The head outputs one gain between 0 and 1 per bin. The vocal estimate is the mixture spectrum multiplied by this mask, so the system attenuates mixture bins rather than generating a new spectrum; it keeps the mixture phase.

Back to audio, one hop later.

The masked spectrum goes through the inverse transform with the short synthesis window, overlap-added with the previous block. The streaming implementation shifts the reconstructed signal by one hop. Waiting for an input block to fill adds up to one more hop minus one sample: 127 samples in the worst case.

Where the compute goes.

The encoder and the mask head run on every bin, so the dual window pays for its 1025 bins where the short window has 129: 5.84 against 1.55 million multiply-adds per frame. These per-bin layers dominate the dual-window cost. Widening the GRU also adds compute, which is tested separately below.

5 · Data and experimental design

Change the window; control the comparison

Having defined the separator, I need to distinguish a window benefit from a training, model-size or compute advantage. The primary comparison holds the architecture, synthesis window and hop fixed within each latency pair. Each configuration is trained separately using the same recipe.

The data and prediction target

I use MUSDB18-HQ [5], whose 150 songs include separate vocals, drums, bass and other-instrument stems. The target is the vocal stem; the other three stems form the accompaniment. I convert the signals to mono at 44.1 kHz.

The official 100-song training set is split into 86 training and 14 validation songs. The official 50-song test set supplies the reported full-song separation results. Validation chooses checkpoints and the analysis-window length; the later learning-rate follow-ups reuse the test set and are identified as exploratory.

86training songslearn the separator
14validation songsselect windows and checkpoints
50test songsevaluate complete performances

Training a comparable system

Each main configuration uses two random seeds and 200,000 updates, training on 3-second crops in batches of 16. AdamW starts at a learning rate of 10−3, with 2,000 warmup updates followed by cosine decay. Every 5,000 updates, full-song validation SI-SDR is measured; the best-validation checkpoint is retained.

Random stem gains, cross-song remixing and 20% vocal-dominant mixtures vary the input balance. The loss is computed on the reconstructed waveform: 10 × L1 plus a multi-resolution spectral term. Its three analysis resolutions are identical for all models, so changing a model’s window does not change the training objective.

The spectral term compares compressed magnitudes (power 0.3) at FFT sizes 512, 1024 and 2048, with hops 128, 256 and 512. Training configuration ↗

Which comparison answers which question?

01 · Primary quality comparison

C vs B at 5.8 ms; D vs E at 2.9 ms. Same architecture, parameter count, hop and synthesis window; different analysis windows. A is the longer-latency reference. Score all 50 complete test songs.

02 · Window mechanism

Apply ideal ratio masks computed from clean stems. Use 30-second validation excerpts and uSDR to choose N from 1024, 2048 and 4096. Then compare test-song oracle SI-SDR gains with learned-model gains. This is a diagnostic reference, not a universal upper bound.

03 · Capacity and compute controls

Retrain B/C with GRU widths 64, 128 and 256. Separately widen B/E until their multiply-adds per frame match C/D within 1%. Check whether the benefit persists when extra arithmetic is spent on the network instead.

04 · Latency and runtime

Use a trained-model prefix test and an identity-mask streaming impulse test to check framing and look-ahead. Time the complete STFT–network–iSTFT pipeline with ONNX Runtime on one CPU thread, reporting P99 compute time, missed deadlines and real-time factor.

05 · Simulated vocal microphone

Mix the band 5 dB below the vocal in 30-second test excerpts. Hold the mixture’s predicted mask fixed to measure retained voice and remaining accompaniment separately. Sweep mask strength for equal-attenuation comparisons; repeat with dry, filtered-and-delayed and synthetic-room accompaniment paths.

How the results are summarized

SI-SDR measures how closely the output matches the clean vocal after allowing an overall gain change; higher is better. I average the two seeds’ scores within each song, subtract one model from the other on that same song, and report the median of those 50 paired differences, the number of songs improved, and a 95% confidence interval from 10,000 song-bootstrap resamples.

Bootstrap intervalResample the 50 songs with replacement 10,000 times and recompute the paired median each time; the middle 95% of those medians is the interval.

This paired median differs from subtracting two overall medians. It describes a typical song while reducing the influence of extreme failures on nearly vocal-free tracks. I also report means and examine those failures; the confidence intervals cover variation across songs, not training-seed uncertainty.

The matched-compute learning-rate checks were prompted by test results: two rates were compared, and the rate was chosen using validation scores. These follow-ups reuse the same test songs, so they provide exploratory evidence. Protocols and follow-up record ↗

The experiments now let me ask, in order: does quality improve, what explains it, is the promised latency realized, can computation keep up, and what happens to the voice in a simulated microphone mixture?

Matched compute

Not just a bigger model.

Widen the short-window network until it costs as many operations per frame, and give it a learning rate chosen on validation data: it closes about a third of the gap. These learning-rate checks were prompted by test results and are exploratory. At 2.9 ms, the dual window still leads by a median 0.50 dB [0.28, 0.63], on 41 of 50 songs. At 5.8 ms, the lead is +0.51 dB [0.40, 0.65], on 42 of 50 songs.

Failure case

One song goes the other way.

On PR – Happy Daze the voice sits 26 dB below the band. The dual window removes more accompaniment but also more voice, and D loses 19 dB against the wider E control shown here. Against the original E, D loses about 20 dB.

That single song is why the matched-compute mean gain (+0.06 dB, 95% CI −0.82 to +0.59) is far below its median (+0.50 dB). For the original D–E pair the mean is +0.35 dB (−0.57 to +0.91), also inconclusive; most songs improve, but the average gain is uncertain.

Model size

Small models benefit too.

Shrinking the GRU to 0.38 M and 0.17 M parameters keeps the gain at +0.59 and +0.54 dB. The 0.17 M dual-window model even beats the 0.94 M short-window model by 0.31 dB per song, though it still costs 3.3× as many multiply-adds per frame. In speech enhancement, asymmetric windows helped a weaker model more [3]; here the gain is slightly smaller for the smallest model, not larger.

6.2 · Results · mechanism

What the window lets a mask recover

Ideal ratio maskA mask computed from the true vocal and accompaniment stems. It shows what a given window pair allows a mask to recover, independent of any trained network.

The quality gain persists across model sizes and matched-compute controls. To probe why, I return to the ideal-mask experiment described above: if the same window pattern appears without a learned estimator, the spectral representation is part of the explanation. Low-frequency vocal-loss measurements provide a second diagnostic.

Fig. 7 · ideal masks

Ideal ratio masks: uSDR on 30-second excerpts of the 14 validation songs. The gain comparison uses full-test-song SI-SDR. These are different evaluation settings. Octave losses: output vocal energy relative to the clean vocal with simulated bleed, 50 test songs. Source ↗

Short windows lose detail even with ideal masks.

With ideal masks, symmetric windows lose about 3 dB between 23.2 and 2.9 ms. This loss appears before a network is trained: the window representation itself matters.

A long analysis window recovers detail.

At 5.8 ms, a 2048-sample analysis window (10.06 dB) beats a symmetric window at twice the latency (9.52 dB at 11.6 ms). Going to 4096 adds nothing, which is why 2048 was chosen, on validation songs only.

The trained models realize about 40% of it.

On the test songs the oracle gain of the dual window over the short window is +1.87 dB, positive on every song. The trained 0.94 M models turn it into +0.76 dB. The remaining gap leaves room to improve the estimator and its training; this experiment does not identify a single cause.

The loss sits in the low voice.

Where does the short window lose? Mostly in the lowest octave of the voice. With simulated bleed, the 256-sample window loses 3.6 dB of vocal energy between 125 and 250 Hz, the dual window 2.6 dB, the long window 2.4 dB.

Resolution is not the whole story: the 128-sample window loses 2.9 dB there, less than 256 samples, and its dual partner 2.2 dB.

6.3 · Results · latency

Latency, measured rather than assumed

The window analysis offers a reason for the quality gain. H2 adds a stricter requirement: that gain must not come from secretly reading more future audio. I therefore check the trained model’s look-ahead and the streaming implementation’s signal delay against the synthesis-window bound.

Fig. 8 · the prefix test

Schematic. The measured look-ahead is the largest over 16 positions of the change within a hop, with the trained weights; the bound is the synthesis window minus one. Source ↗

Two inputs that agree, then differ.

Feed the trained model two signals that are identical up to sample n and different afterwards. Compare the outputs aligned to the original signal, before adding the streaming delay. If their first difference is at n − L, output at that position depends on input L samples later.

Where the outputs part is the look-ahead.

The distance from the first changed output to sample n measures look-ahead in the aligned output. Repeat at 16 change positions within a hop and take the largest distance. For the 2.9 ms models it is 123 samples, under the bound of 127; for 5.8 ms, 247 under 255. The 2048-sample analysis window did not add a single sample.

And the frame-by-frame code agrees.

An impulse fed through the streaming code with an identity mask comes out exactly one hop later; with the wait for an input block to fill, the worst case is the synthesis window minus one sample: 127 samples, 2.88 ms. What is not measured: the audio interface’s own buffers.

6.4 · Results · runtime

Before the next hop arrives

The latency tests confirm the framing bound, but they do not tell me whether computation can keep up with incoming audio. The next measurement times the full STFT–network–inverse-STFT pipeline on one Xeon Gold 6150 thread. All five configurations are benchmarked in the same job with ONNX Runtime.

Real-time factor is processing time divided by audio duration; below 1 means the system keeps up on average. P99 is the time within which 99% of measured hops finish. I also count hops that exceed their deadline, because an average can hide individual stalls.

Fig. 9 · the deadline

one hop = one slot of 1.45 mstime spent computing itfinished after the deadline

Mean time per hop from the benchmark (one Xeon Gold 6150 thread, all models in one job), shown in slow motion. Source ↗

What a hop is.

At 2.9 ms latency a new block of 64 samples arrives every 1.45 ms. Each slot on the track is one such hop. Whatever the model computes for a block has to be finished before the slot ends, or the audio glitches.

Half the budget.

The 2.9 ms dual-window model needs 0.75 ms of its 1.45 ms on average and 0.76 ms at the 99th percentile. 0.01% of hops were late.

The price is compute, not latency.

The 128-sample window alone needs half that time. At the same latency the dual window roughly doubles CPU time: real-time factor 0.515 against 0.262.

Implementation matters.

The same model run in eager PyTorch takes 1.7 ms per hop (real-time factor 1.18) and misses every deadline. A deployed system would run in a dedicated real-time audio thread.

The whole trade-off on one chart.

Lowering latency from 23.2 to 5.8 ms costs 1.05 dB with a short window but only 0.31 dB with the dual window, and 2.9 ms costs another 0.16 dB. What the dual window buys with quality it pays in multiply-adds per frame, about 3.8× to 4.7× its short-window baseline.

6.5 · Results · vocal preservation

The vocal microphone

The quality and timing results address full music mixtures. To move closer to the motivating use case, I also ask whether the dual window preserves more voice in a simulated vocal microphone, where the band leaks into a vocal-dominant signal. In a simulated version, studio stems are mixed with the accompaniment 5 dB below the voice, and the mixture determines a mask. Holding that mask fixed and applying it separately to voice and accompaniment splits the output exactly into the voice kept and the bleed remaining, in the spirit of Hagerman and Olofsson’s measurement method [6].

Fig. 10 · bleed

Simulated bleed from digitally mixed stems, 30-second excerpts of the 50 test songs; sweeping the mask strength traces each curve. The three paths are fixed synthetic transformations, not real rooms or microphones. Source ↗

Two things to trade.

A separator can always remove more of the band by removing more of everything. The fair comparison is at equal bleed attenuation: how much voice does each model lose while removing the same amount of band?

At 2.9 ms, the dual window keeps more voice.

Sweeping the mask strength traces each model’s curve. At a common 8.9 dB of bleed attenuation the dual window loses 0.67 dB of vocal energy, the short window 0.97 dB; at 5.8 ms, 0.68 against 0.94 dB at a common 9.7 dB attenuation. Each pair uses its own operating point.

And it survives a change of path.

With frozen models, the accompaniment was sent through three fixed paths before mixing: dry, mild filtering plus delay, and a synthetic room. The paired gain of the dual window stays between +0.59 and +0.91 dB on every path. At equal attainable attenuation, D keeps 0.32–0.35 dB more vocal energy than E across the three paths; 48 of 50 songs have a shared attenuation range.

7 · Conclusion

More spectral history is a useful way to spend compute

In this controlled music-vocal setting, the dual window recovers quality on most songs while retaining the short synthesis window’s algorithmic latency. The results support all three hypotheses within the tested configurations:

H1

A long analysis window with a short synthesis window recovers quality at the short window’s latency.

+0.76 dB per song at 5.8 ms and +0.73 dB at 2.9 ms; 45 of 50 songs improve at each latency.

H2

Extending the analysis window costs compute, not latency.

Measured look-ahead 247 ≤ 255 and 123 ≤ 127 samples, unchanged by the long window; about 2× the CPU time of the short-window baseline.

H3

The gain does not depend on a large model.

+0.54 to +0.76 dB from 0.17 M to 0.94 M parameters.

My main conclusion is that the representation deserves a place alongside network size in low-latency separator design. In the exploratory matched-compute study, allocating arithmetic to a longer analysis window still leads by about 0.5 dB per song over wider short-window models. The benefit is not explained simply by parameter count.

For a design decision, C recovers much of the quality lost when moving from 23.2 to 5.8 ms: the paired gap to A is 0.31 dB, compared with 1.05 dB for B. Moving from C to D halves algorithmic latency again and costs another 0.16 dB per song, while doubling the number of frames processed each second.

The evidence supports a quality–latency–compute trade-off, with a real failure on a very quiet vocal. It does not establish perceptual superiority or readiness for a live monitoring system. Those boundaries determine the next experiments.

8 · Limitations and next steps

Where the evidence ends

One dataset, one target, mono audio
The study covers music vocals on MUSDB18-HQ. It does not establish generalization to other datasets, instrument targets or stereo production. Further evaluation should cross those boundaries.
Objective scores without a listening study
SI-SDR is the primary endpoint; spectral and preservation diagnostics help interpret it. There is no controlled human listening test, so a numerical gain cannot establish a perceptual preference. The demo below illustrates outputs.
A typical-song gain with severe exceptions
At 2.9 ms, the mean paired gain’s confidence interval includes zero, despite a positive median and 45 of 50 songs improving. The quiet-vocal failure is a real preservation problem; vocal-active-only scoring does not make it disappear.
Limited model and training coverage
There are two seeds per configuration, one small architecture family, and GRU-width variations. Song-bootstrap intervals omit seed uncertainty. The two-rate matched-compute checks were exploratory and reused the test set; an independent evaluation would strengthen that evidence.
Algorithmic latency is not end-to-end latency
Timing was measured on one CPU type and thread in a shared environment, with occasional late hops. Audio-interface buffers, scheduling under live load, embedded targets and acoustic feedback remain untested.
Digitally simulated microphones and rooms
The bleed tests use studio stems and three fixed feed-forward paths. Their success does not demonstrate robustness to real microphones, arbitrary rooms or a feedback loop. A next step is a measured acoustic setup with end-to-end timing and listening evaluation.

With these limits in view, the listening desk lets you inspect the trade-off directly: keep the musical moment fixed and change the separator.
