A causal vocal separator that answers within 5.8 or 2.9 milliseconds, and a window design that wins back much of the quality that short windows lose.

01 / Problem

The latency budget

Live sound, in-ear monitoring and hearing devices need the voice separated from the band within a few milliseconds. Offline separators such as HTDemucs read seconds of future audio. A causal separator cannot, and in a short-time Fourier transform the window decides how long it has to wait.

How much quality does a causal separator lose at 5.8 or 2.9 ms, and can the window design win part of it back without adding latency?

Fig. 2 · windows

Top: the signal arriving in slow motion; the windows end at the present moment and the bright part of the tape is what the model sees. Bottom: a synthetic sung note (white ticks) and accompaniment partials (gray ticks) seen through the current analysis window; dots mark STFT bins.

1024 samples

A window is a wait.

An STFT separator can process a frame only once all of its samples have arrived. At 44.1 kHz, a 1024-sample window means waiting 23.2 ms: too long for a singer’s in-ear monitor.

In return its frequency bins are 43 Hz apart, and every harmonic of the sung note stands on its own.

256 samples

Cut the wait, lose the detail.

A 256-sample window answers within 5.8 ms. Its bins are 172 Hz apart, and neighboring partials of the voice and the accompaniment start to merge.

128 samples

At 2.9 ms, a blur.

With 128 samples the bins are 345 Hz apart. Below 1.6 kHz, four bins have to describe the whole mixture, and a mask can no longer tell the voice from the band.

02 / Idea

Look back long, answer short.

A dual window splits the two jobs (Mauler & Martin, 2007). A 2048-sample analysis window gives the model a sharp view of the recent past; a 128-sample synthesis window decides when the output can leave.

The spectrum sharpens again, and the wait stays at 2.9 ms.

Nothing is lost on the way out.

The synthesis window is chosen so that analysis × synthesis is a short Hann window. Overlapping those every 64 samples adds up to exactly one, so the signal is reconstructed perfectly.

The latency is measured, not assumed.

Feeding the trained models inputs that differ only after a given sample shows how far each output looks ahead: at most 123 samples at 2.9 ms (bound 127) and 247 at 5.8 ms (bound 255). The long window adds no latency.

03 / Results

Fifty songs, one network

The same small network handles every window: a frequency encoder, one causal GRU layer and a mask head, 0.94 M parameters. Within each latency the hop and the synthesis window are identical; only the analysis window differs. Every model was trained twice on MUSDB18-HQ and scored on all 50 test songs.

Fig. 3 · per-song gain

One bar per MUSDB18-HQ test song, two training seeds averaged, sorted by gain. Bars above the line are songs the dual window improves. Gains below −3 dB are clipped and marked.

5.8 ms

45 of 50 songs improve.

Each bar is a test song, raised by how much vocal SI-SDR the dual window gains over the 256-sample window at the same latency. Median gain per song: +0.76 dB (95% CI 0.57 to 0.90).

2.9 ms

The same at half the latency.

Against the 128-sample window: +0.73 dB (0.59 to 0.88), again on 45 of 50 songs. Halving the latency costs the dual window only 0.16 dB.

Matched compute

Not just a bigger model.

Widen the short-window network until it costs as many operations per frame, and give it a learning rate chosen on validation data: it closes about a third of the gap. The dual window still leads by a median 0.50 dB, on 41 of 50 songs.

Failure case

One song goes the other way.

On PR – Happy Daze the voice sits 26 dB below the band. The dual window removes more accompaniment but also more voice, and the song loses 19 dB.

That single song is why the mean gain (+0.06 dB) is far below the median: the result holds for most songs, not on average.

04 / Cost

Before the next hop arrives

Algorithmic latency is only half of the story: every hop must also be computed before the next one arrives. The full pipeline (STFT, network, inverse STFT) was exported to ONNX Runtime and timed on a single CPU thread.

Fig. 4 · the deadline

Mean time per hop from the benchmark (one Xeon Gold 6150 thread, all models in one job), shown in slow motion.

Half the budget.

The 2.9 ms dual-window model has 1.45 ms per hop and needs 0.75 ms on average, 0.76 ms at the 99th percentile. 0.01% of hops were late.

The price is compute, not latency.

The 128-sample window alone needs half that time. At the same latency the dual window roughly doubles CPU time: real-time factor 0.515 against 0.262.

Implementation matters.

The same model run in eager PyTorch takes 1.7 ms per hop (real-time factor 1.18) and misses every deadline.

Limitations

  • Mono vocals on one dataset (MUSDB18-HQ), scored with SI-SDR; no listening test.
  • Small models trained with one recipe, two seeds per configuration.
  • Gains are medians over songs; at 2.9 ms the mean gain is not significant.
  • Timing on one CPU type; end-to-end latency through an audio interface was not measured.
  • The desk uses excerpts of ccMixter songs, not MUSDB18 audio; the bleed scene is simulated.
